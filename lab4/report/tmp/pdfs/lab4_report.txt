                                              Summary
          Polynomial trajectory optimisation connects waypoint geometry to the con-
      tinuous state references required for quadrotor flight. This laboratory derives the
      single-segment minimum-velocity solutions, counts the conditions for multi-segment
      minimum-snap trajectories, and implements a ROS trajectory follower for the official
      Unity racing course. With endpoint positions alone, the optimum is P (t) = t and
      its cost is 1 for all three polynomial degrees considered. A prescribed terminal
      velocity changes the solution: the cubic reduces the quadratic cost from 4 to 17/8.
      In the recorded Unity run, the quadrotor approached all 12 gate centres in order
      while tracking a 91.08 s reference trajectory. The active-flight position error had a
      root-mean-square (RMS) value of 0.319 m, and the final position was 0.169 m from
      the last gate centre.


1     Lab introduction and team information
The task is to convert a sequence of gate poses into a smooth flight reference and
track it through the official simulated course. The exercises combine two analytical
deliverables with a drone-racing implementation[1]. This report follows that sequence:
quadratic programming explains how endpoint constraints shape a polynomial; multi-
segment analysis explains how adjacent pieces join; the flight experiment shows how the
resulting reference interacts with a feedback controller.
The implemented system is shown in Figure 1. Gate positions and orientations are supplied
by the course map. The trajectory generator produces desired states, and the controller
from Lab 3 closes the loop through the TESSE bridge. Team names, student numbers,
group information and individual contributions will be added after the author details are
confirmed.

Euler–Lagrange gives Pi = 0 on every open segment. Each piece therefore has degree
at most seven and eight coefficients, giving 8k unknowns. Assume that position, velocity,
acceleration and jerk are fixed at the overall start and end. The conditions determining
the stationary optimum are listed in Table 1.
Table 1: Scalar condition count for k minimum-snap segments. The free-derivative row
combines continuity constraints with natural optimality conditions.

Type               Conditions                                                          Count
Waypoint           Fix both ends of each piece to the appropriate waypoint.                2k

There are k + 1 physical waypoints. Each interior waypoint supplies two position equations,
one for each adjacent piece, while each overall endpoint supplies one. For k = 3, the counts
are 6 waypoint, 12 free-derivative and 6 fixed-derivative equations, totalling 24.
The derivative map in Figure 4 shows where these conditions act. Each interior position
is imposed on both adjacent pieces, whereas the free derivatives share a common value
across the knot.
Velocity, acceleration and jerk continuity define admissible trajectories. Their common
values at interior knots remain free. Minimising snap then supplies continuity of derivatives
four, five and six as natural optimality conditions. The seventh derivative may jump
because the interior positions are prescribed. Thus a QP needs 2k + 3(k − 1) + 6 = 5k + 3
explicit equality constraints; optimisation supplies the remaining 3(k − 1) conditions.
Treating all 8k equations as prescribed derivative values would remove the distinction
between feasibility and optimality.

3.1.1              Part 0: first-gate reference

The simple planner takes the first gate pose, normalises its quaternion and publishes
a desired state with zero linear and angular velocity and acceleration. A 0.01 s timer
keeps the command active and broadcasts the desired pose in the world frame. Empty
lists and invalid first poses are rejected. The saved hover record contains the position
(0.423, 3.165, 2.374) m; this individual sample is not used to estimate a settling time or a
steady-state error statistic.

3.1.2              Part 1.1: initialise from the current state

The follower obtains position and yaw from /current_state and waits for a valid state
before planning. Waypoints received first are retained until that state arrives. The initial
vertex is the measured position, which joins the current vehicle location to the first gate.

Once a trajectory exists, repeated waypoint messages do not restart it.

3.1.3   Part 1.2: optimise position and yaw

The generator builds a three-dimensional position trajectory and a scalar yaw trajectory
using identical segment durations. Interior vertices constrain the gate position and yaw.
Successive yaw angles are unwrapped using
                                                                       
                    ψi = ψi−1 + atan2 sin(ψ̄i − ψi−1 ), cos(ψ̄i − ψi−1 ) ,              (19)

where ψ̄i is the yaw extracted from the gate quaternion. This selects the short angular
increment across the ±π boundary.
Both optimisers minimise snap using ten coefficients per segment, so their polynomials
have degree at most nine. The library shares endpoint derivatives through order four at
interior vertices. Calls to makeStartOrEnd(..., SNAP) set derivatives one through four
to zero at the overall endpoints. This implementation therefore includes endpoint snap
constraints and uses a different polynomial space from the minimal degree-seven derivation
in Deliverable 2. The mathematical count there applies to the stated endpoint-through-jerk
problem.
The segment-time estimator uses gate spacing with parameters vmax = 2 m s−1 and
amax = 1 m s−2 . A time scale of 1 is applied and every segment is at least 0.5 s long. These
parameters choose durations; the linear QP does not impose them as hard velocity or
acceleration bounds.

3.1.4   Part 1.3: publish the desired state

The generator evaluates position, velocity, acceleration, yaw, yaw rate and yaw acceleration
at the elapsed ROS time. The sample time is clipped to [0, T ], where T is the trajectory
duration. The final rest reference therefore continues after T . The desired-state timer is
configured at 100 Hz; the controller timer is configured at 200 Hz. These settings describe
requested timer rates, not measured throughput of the simulator.

4     Discussion
4.1    Polynomial freedom and boundary conditions
The single-segment solutions show when a higher polynomial degree is useful. With
position constraints alone, constant velocity already reaches the global lower bound. The
terminal-velocity condition changes the feasible set: a cubic can redistribute velocity over
the interval and reduce the cost from 4 to 17/8. The overshoot in Figure 2 follows from
arriving at the final point with negative velocity. Boundary conditions therefore determine
the value of extra coefficients more directly than polynomial degree alone.
For multiple segments, the distinction between explicit constraints and natural conditions
explains how the optimiser chooses free waypoint derivatives. In the flight implementation,
shared position and yaw segment times keep the reference components synchronised,
while angle unwrapping avoids a full-turn interpolation at a representation discontinuity.
Endpoint sampling then gives the controller a persistent rest reference after the planned
motion ends.


4.2    What the flight measurements establish
The recorded run supports ordered gate-centre visits and quantifies tracking over the
course. Centre proximity is a spatial diagnostic: a collision-free crossing of every gate
would require crossing geometry or simulator event records in addition to these distances.
Likewise, 91.08 s is the reference duration, not an independently measured official race
time. The optional speed challenge is outside the demonstrated result.
The terminal trace shows continued position variation after the reference stops. A time-
window settling criterion would be needed to quantify sustained hover accuracy. No

controller comparison is available to attribute that variation to a particular gain, bridge
timing effect or simulator model. The present results therefore describe the selected
configuration and recorded run. They provide a concrete starting point for any subsequent
time-allocation or gain study.


5     Conclusions
The analytical exercises connect polynomial coefficients to boundary conditions and identify
the continuity and stationarity conditions for multi-segment planning. The implemented
ROS follower extends that formulation to spatial waypoints and unwrapped yaw, then
supplies the derivatives needed by the Lab 3 controller. In the official Unity recording, the
vehicle approached all 12 gate centres in order with an active-flight RMS position error of
0.319 m. Together, the derivations and recorded flight demonstrate the complete chain
from waypoint constraints to a tracked reference trajectory.
